People analytics, from raw tables to recommendations

The brief I set myself: you are the HR lead of a ~200-person company. Why are people leaving, what is it costing, and what should you do first? I answered it the way I build people data at work: define the KPIs, clean the data once, prove the numbers reconcile, then build the dashboard.

§ 2

Approach

StepWhat I did
1 · Define the KPIs Wrote down headcount, attrition, regrettable and early exits, 12-month retention and cost of attrition before building anything, so every chart uses the same definition.
2 · Clean once Built three SQL models in Metabase as the single source of truth: trimmed labels, fixed typos, classified exits, flagged a conflicting record. The raw tables are never edited.
3 · Reconcile Rebuilt monthly headcount from individual records and compared it to the source's own table: it matches at month end in 136 of 136 months, which pins down an undocumented definition.
4 · Set the rules Group sizes on every rate, rates hidden below 20 people, confidence intervals on risk drivers, managers anonymised and no group under 5 people singled out.
5 · Answer, then show An executive summary leads with the cost and four findings; the other tabs hold the evidence, with filters to drill in.
§ 3

Findings

$285kyearly cost of attrition, about 2% of payroll (at a 30% replacement-cost assumption)
13.5%attrition in Production vs 7.5% in the rest of the company; closing the gap is worth ~$84k a year
31%of leavers quit within their first year
51%of the 2011 hiring wave still employed after 3 years, vs 82–89% for later cohorts

What I would recommend

  1. Production retention review: stay interviews, shift patterns and pay versus market for technician roles, targeting the rest-of-company rate.
  2. Structured onboarding: 30/60/90-day check-ins, with 12-month retention tracked by hiring cohort.
  3. Manager support: start with the three teams that lost more than half of everyone they managed.
  4. Data hygiene: adopt end-of-month headcount as the official definition and block conflicting records at entry.

A separate public sample (IBM HR Analytics, 1,470 employees) shows the risk drivers: people working overtime were 2.9× as likely to leave (95% CI 2.3–3.7).

§ 4

Data & caveats

All data is public sample data about fictional people; no employer's data is used. The company dataset covers 310 employees, 2006 to April 2017. Cost figures depend on the replacement-cost assumption, which viewers can change on the dashboard. Findings are associations, not proof of cause.
